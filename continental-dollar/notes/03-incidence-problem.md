# The incidence problem — read before writing any welfare claim

## What I got wrong initially

The first design (`01`, and the first version of `02`) assumed the depreciation tables were
the instrument by which legislatures expropriated creditors, so that the gap between the
legislated scale and the market rate could be read directly as a transfer. **That is too
quick, and in places backwards.**

Grubb (2023: 97) states the mechanism precisely:

> "Depreciation tables were now used to adjust the pay-off value **between the borrowing
> date and repayment date** for both principal and interest combined, so that what was
> repaid was comparable in present value to the principal borrowed and interest owed."

Two consequences:

1. **The tables were a remedy, not the injury.** The injury was legal tender at par — being
   forced to accept a depreciated Continental as though it were a specie dollar. That is
   what Smith condemns in *WN*. The depreciation scales were the states' attempt to
   *undo* that, by restating nominal Continental sums in real terms.
2. **What matters is the distortion to the ratio, not the level.** Compensation depends on
   `S_r / S_b` — the scale at repayment over the scale at borrowing. A scale can be wrong
   in level at every date and still deliver the correct ratio; or be nearly right in level
   and badly wrong in ratio.

## Why the direction cannot be signed without specifying the application

| Application | Who is settling with whom | Effect of under-recognizing depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| **Private contracts** in Continentals | Debtor repays creditor | Scale converts nominal to specie. Under-recognizing depreciation at the contracting date credits the sum with *more* specie value → debtor pays more → **creditor gains** |
| **Loan office certificates** | Government repays lender | Compensation ≈ principal / S_b. Under-recognizing at the borrowing date → lender credited with more than he lent → **lender gains** |
| **Soldiers' depreciation pay** | State compensates soldier for wages paid in depreciated money | Under-recognizing depreciation says his wages were worth more than they were → state owes *less* back pay → **soldier loses** |

So the same arithmetic sign flips welfare direction depending on which settlement is in
view. Any claim of the form "state X expropriated its creditors by Y" requires naming the
settlement, the dates, and the direction. **Do not write such a sentence without doing that
work.**

## What the measured gap does support, without any incidence claim

- Thirteen legislatures assigned **materially different legal values** to the same national
  currency on the same date — a 1.58x mean and 2.50x maximum spread across the three
  regional groups.
- Those values **deviated systematically and persistently** from the one contemporaneous
  market series available, in a pattern stable enough to be structural rather than random.
- Congress's own scale was **the most distorted of all** in 1777 (recognition ratio 0.37),
  and converged toward the market only as the currency approached collapse.

These are facts about the *degree of legislative discretion over the real value of debts*.
That is the paper's institutional finding, and it does not depend on signing the transfer.

## The live question the paper should pose

Is the dispersion **real** or **political**?

- **Bullock (1895: 132) argues real:** government spending was "very unequally distributed
  in the various parts of the country," circulation was slow, so "there was no general
  uniformity in the depreciation"; and "the depreciation was naturally greatest at the seat
  of the federal treasury." On this reading the scales differ because *prices* differed.
- **Grubb (2023: 97, 140–41) argues political:** the tables "do not measure the current value
  of the Continental dollar per se"; they were built after the fact by legislatures;
  variation "would be consistent with political motivations affecting the values chosen
  rather than the tables reflecting true market values."

**Neither has adjudicated it, and adjudicating it requires exactly what neither used:
state-level market prices set against state-level legislated scales.** That is the paper's
research question, and it is honestly open.

With three regional groups and a single Philadelphia benchmark, this draft can pose the
question sharply and bound it — it cannot fully resolve it. Say so. The resolution needs
the state-level series from *American State Papers*, Finance V: 773 et seq., plus regional
price evidence, and is the natural extension.

## Caveat that must appear in the paper

Group 2 (PA/NJ/DE/MD/VA) contains Pennsylvania, and the market benchmark is a **Philadelphia**
merchant's books. Group 2's near-perfect tracking (ratio ≈ 0.98–1.09) is therefore partly
definitional and must not be reported as a finding. The result that survives is the
**gradient**: the two groups distant from Philadelphia — New England/New York and the
Carolinas — both under-recognize by a similar margin (≈0.7), which is consistent with either
explanation and is what motivates the question.
